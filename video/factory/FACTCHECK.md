# Fact check: "Extending human intelligence" (the thinking-factory video)

| Claim in the video | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open: "Seventeen doctors. Three years. And still no answer for her little boy. Then, one night, she found a way to think bigger." | TODAY (Sept 2023), "A boy saw 17 doctors over 3 years for chronic pain. ChatGPT found the diagnosis"; also told on NEJM AI Grand Rounds ("Partners in diagnosis: ChatGPT, a mother's intuition and a doctor's expertise") | Tagged "A TRUE STORY"; the mother and son are only shown as silhouettes. On-screen source: "TODAY, Sept 2023 · NEJM AI Grand Rounds". |
| Payoff: "His mother typed every symptom into an AI, and it suggested tethered cord syndrome. A neurosurgeon checked the scans, and agreed. Alex had surgery." | Same TODAY report: the mother entered Alex's symptoms and MRI notes into ChatGPT, which suggested tethered cord syndrome; a neurosurgeon reviewed the MRI and agreed; Alex had surgery | "The AI didn't replace her doctors. It gave her the reach to find the right one" - it produced a hypothesis; the specialist confirmed and treated. |
| "Three hours digging through fifty reports, and no time left to think." / "Three hours of searching become three hours of thinking." | The client's brief (the example of fifty reports and three hours) | Illustrative, as in the brief. |
| On screen: "office workers spend 1.8 hours a day just searching · McKinsey" | McKinsey Global Institute (2012), *The social economy*: employees spend 1.8 hours every day (9.3 hours a week) searching and gathering information | |
| Newsreel: "Consultants: 40% better work" / on screen "on tasks AI is good at · HBS & BCG study, 2023" | Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier" (Harvard Business School working paper 24-013, 2023): 758 BCG consultants; on tasks inside the frontier, those with GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with more than 40% higher quality | The on-screen line states the condition (tasks inside AI's capability). |
| Newsreel: "Writers: 40% faster" / on screen "40% faster, 18% better · professional writing tasks · Science, 2023" | Noy & Zhang, "Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence", *Science* 381 (2023): time taken fell 40%, output quality rose 18% | |
| Newsreel: "And beginners gain the most!" / on screen "+34% · customer support agents · QJE, 2025" | Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 140(2), 2025: +14% issues resolved per hour on average, +34% for novice and low-skilled agents | |
| Interviews: "It read my loan papers to me in plain English." / "It argues with my essay before my teacher does." | Illustrative uses (explain / translate jargon; critique an argument) | Tagged "RE-ENACTMENT" and with the use each one shows. |
| "Trust it blindly, and you stop checking." / card "TRUST IT BLINDLY → you check less · Microsoft & Carnegie Mellon survey, 2025" | Lee et al. (Microsoft Research & Carnegie Mellon), "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking", CHI 2025: survey of 319 knowledge workers (936 examples) - higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less critical thinking; higher self-confidence with more | Worded as an association from a survey ("you check less"), not as a measured causal effect; the card says "survey". |
| "In one study, consultants using AI on a task it was bad at were nineteen points less likely to get it right." / card "19 POINTS less likely to be right, on a task AI was bad at · Harvard Business School & BCG study, 2023" | Dell'Acqua et al. 2023: on a task designed to fall outside the frontier, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions than those without AI | Percentage points, as stated in the paper. |
| "Used well, you think more." / the host's rule "My machines think WITH you. Never FOR you." | The client's brief ("This does not necessarily replace thinking. Used well, it can increase the amount and depth of thinking") | |
| The thirteen uses of an intellectual amplifier | The client's brief | Each is stamped on screen, numbered "USE n OF 13", as it is acted out in the rooms: Untangler (explain unfamiliar subjects, translate jargon); Press (summarize documents, find inconsistencies); Arguing Mirror (simulate opposing views, compare competing ideas, critique an argument); Kitchen of What-If (generate hypotheses, explore consequences, brainstorm, organize thoughts, turn a vague idea into a plan, identify missing questions). They are also listed on each room's door plaque. |
| The personal scenario: your dad (on a diabetes pill, numb feet in both feet); the letter's jargon untangled; fifty studies pressed to six, with a flag where two disagree; the case against each treatment and the hole in your own reasoning ("blame the diabetes, so nothing to do"); what else could explain it, what happens if you wait; a one-page plan with three questions; the question "Could his diabetes pill be lowering his B12?"; the doctor: "That's exactly the right question"; "One blood test. His B12 was low. A supplement, and by spring, the numbness is fading." | Dramatization built on real medicine: metformin, the most common diabetes pill, is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency, which can cause or worsen peripheral neuropathy; the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care (2025) recommend periodic B12 measurement in people taking metformin, especially with peripheral neuropathy; B12 deficiency neuropathy can improve with replacement, and untreated nerve damage can become permanent | Tagged "DRAMATIZATION". The letter's plain translations are real definitions (peripheral neuropathy = damaged nerves in the hands or feet; bilateral = on both sides; idiopathic = no known cause; paresthesia = pins and needles; prognosis = what happens next; benign = not cancer). The two studies on the flag are labelled "illustrative". |
| "The benefit isn't speed. It's reach." | The client's brief ("The benefit is not merely speed. It is greater cognitive reach.") | |
