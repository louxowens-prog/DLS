# Fact check: "The Hundredth Room" (People may trust AI too much)

Nora is an invented character, and her night is a dramatisation. Her symptoms (a tight chest, an aching jaw, nausea, a
cold sweat at 2 a.m.) are the American Heart Association's list of heart-attack signs that women are more likely to have
and more likely to blame on acid reflux. The AI's answer is the client's own example line ("Based on the patient's
symptoms, this condition is unlikely to be serious"), given to a leading question ("probably just reflux, right?").
The studies and cases below are real, and the film words them the way they report.

| On screen / spoken | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| "Room 98. A woman's life savings. All of it." | Dramatisation (another guest), illustrating the client's example "This investment is safe" | |
| "Nora asks, hoping. And these machines tend to agree with you." | Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" (Anthropic, ICLR 2024): five state-of-the-art AI assistants consistently tended to tell users what they wanted to hear, including agreeing with a user's stated belief | "Tend to" is the study's finding, not "always". |
| "It sounds just as sure when it's wrong." | Steyvers et al., *Nature Machine Intelligence* 2025, "What large language models know and what people think they know": people overestimated how accurate LLM answers were, and longer explanations raised their confidence even when the answer was no more likely to be right | A wrong answer comes in the same fluent style as a right one. |
| "In one study, people trusted low-accuracy AI medical advice as much as a doctor's, and said they'd follow it." Placard: "LOW-ACCURACY AI ADVICE / trusted like a doctor's" | Shekar et al., *NEJM AI* 2025 (MIT Media Lab), 300 participants: low-accuracy AI responses were rated about as valid, trustworthy and complete as doctors' responses, and participants showed a high tendency to follow the potentially harmful advice | |
| "In 2023, two New York lawyers trusted it, and handed a judge six court cases the AI had invented. Fined $5,000." | *Mata v. Avianca*, S.D.N.Y., order of June 22, 2023 (Judge P. Kevin Castel): six cited decisions generated by ChatGPT were bogus; the two lawyers and their firm were sanctioned $5,000 | |
| "Even professional legal AI tools got at least one question in six wrong." Placard: "1 in 6+" | Magesh et al., Stanford RegLab / HAI 2024: Lexis+ AI and Ask Practical Law AI produced incorrect or misgrounded ("hallucinated") answers on more than 17% of queries, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research on more than 34% (17-33% in the HAI summary); general chatbots hallucinated 58-82% of the time on legal queries (earlier RegLab study) | "At least 1 in 6" = the best tool's rate. |
| "No investment is risk-free." | Every investment carries some risk (U.S. SEC, Investor.gov: "all investments involve some degree of risk") | |
| "In 2024, Americans reported losing $5.7 billion to investment scams. More than any other fraud." | U.S. Federal Trade Commission, Consumer Sentinel data for 2024 (released March 2025): $12.5 billion reported lost to fraud in total; investment scams $5.7 billion, the highest of any category | Reported losses. |
| "This has a name. Automation bias. We defer to the machine, because we assume it knows better." | Parasuraman & Manzey, *Human Factors* 2010; the standard definition of automation bias (and complacency) | |
| "With a correct AI hint, radiologists scored about 80%. With a wrong one, the least experienced scored 20%. Even the most experienced: 46%." Chart: % rated correctly, correct hint vs wrong hint, least and most experienced | Dratsch et al., *Radiology* 2023 (27 radiologists reading mammograms with a purported AI's BI-RADS suggestions): correct ratings when the AI was right vs wrong - inexperienced 79.7% vs 19.8%; very experienced 82.3% vs 45.5% | A comparison between the AI's correct and incorrect suggestions, worded that way (not a decline over time). |
| "In simulator studies, the more reliable the system, the more people trusted it, and the more of its failures they missed." | Bailey & Scerbo, *Theoretical Issues in Ergonomics Science* 2007 (monitoring simulated aircraft displays): trust rose as system reliability rose, and as trust rose, monitoring performance (catching failures) fell; realistic, high reliability severely impaired monitoring. Also Molloy & Parasuraman, *Human Factors* 1996: failure detection was poor even when automation failed only once | |
| "Wrong half the time? You'd check everything. Right 99 times in 100? You stop checking." | The client's own framing; consistent with the complacency findings above | The 99% is a thought experiment: the real error rates above are far higher. |
| "It was a heart attack. You came in time." "Women often feel it as jaw pain, nausea, a tight chest, and blame reflux. Don't wait. Call." | American Heart Association, "Heart Attack Symptoms in Women": chest pain is the most common symptom, but women are somewhat more likely to have shortness of breath, nausea/vomiting and back or jaw pain, and often put symptoms down to acid reflux, the flu or aging; call 911, don't wait | End card: "Chest pain, jaw pain, nausea, cold sweat? Call emergency services. Not a chatbot." |
| Background (not in the film): Kabir et al., CHI 2024 (Purdue): 52% of ChatGPT answers to programming questions contained incorrect information, yet participants preferred them 35% of the time, citing their comprehensive, well-articulated style, and overlooked the misinformation 39% of the time | | Supports "confidence mistaken for competence". |
