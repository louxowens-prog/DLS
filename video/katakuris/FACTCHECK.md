# Fact check: "Automation can magnify a small error" (Katakuris-style video)

| Claim in the video | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open: "A computer made forty thousand fraud accusations. Most of them were wrong." | Michigan Unemployment Insurance Agency's MiDAS system: from Oct 2013 to Sep 2015 it adjudicated 40,195 fraud cases by algorithm alone; about 85% of those determinations were wrong (reviews reported by IEEE Spectrum; a separate review of ~22,000 cases found 93% involved no fraud) | "Most" = 85%. |
| One person processing one claim wrong = one error; a system processing 10 million claims with the same mistake = millions of errors | The client's brief (illustration) | Shown with Grandpa's one wrong stamp and the machine that copies his rules. |
| "Automation multiplies everything: what you do right, and what you do wrong." | The client's brief ("Automation multiplies both competence and incompetence") | |
| "The errors aren't random. The same wrong rule hits the same kind of person, again and again, at machine speed." | Added: systematic (correlated) errors, as in MiDAS (one flawed automated rule applied to everyone) and Robodebt (income averaging applied to everyone with irregular income) | Added because it is why automated mistakes pile up on the same people. |
| The personal story (told as "you"): you lose your job, file for unemployment insurance; months later a letter says a computer decided you committed fraud; no human checked; you must repay plus a penalty four times as big plus interest; your wages are garnished and your tax refund seized | MiDAS: fraud determinations made without human review; UIA typically assessed a penalty of four times the amount plus interest; collections by wage garnishment and tax-refund interception | Marked DRAMATIZATION on screen; the dollar figure ($8,000 -> $40,000) is labelled ILLUSTRATIVE AMOUNT. |
| "This happened in Michigan. An algorithm alone decided over forty thousand fraud cases. About eighty-five percent were wrong." | As above; the state later paid a $20 million settlement (Bauserman v. Unemployment Insurance Agency, agreed 2022, approved 2024) | On screen: "40,000+ · DECIDED BY ALGORITHM", "85% WRONG", "Michigan · MiDAS · 2013–2015". |
| "People lost their homes." | Reported victims faced wage garnishment; some lost homes, some went bankrupt (Bridge Michigan; Ford School explainer) | |
| "Australia's Robodebt hit hundreds of thousands more." | Robodebt (2016–2019) raised debts by averaging annual tax-office income across fortnights; the government later refunded or wiped about 470,000 debts raised in error (A$751 million, 2020 settlement); Royal Commission (2023): "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal" | On screen "470,000 · DEBTS REFUNDED" (the debts the government repaid or wiped after conceding the income-averaging method was unlawful). |
| The duet: "Ninety-nine point nine percent... Only one in a thousand wrong... What could possibly go wrong?" | Same arithmetic as below (99.9% right = 1 in 1,000 wrong) | Sung setup for the next line. |
| "Now give the machine a billion decisions. Point one percent of a billion is one million. One million failures." | Arithmetic: 1,000,000,000 × 0.1% = 1,000,000. The line scales the family's machine (10 million claims a year) up to the size of the biggest automated systems | |
| "A million bad ads? You shrug." | The client's brief (ads, recommendations, translations may be manageable) | |
| "One auto-translation turned good morning into this. Attack them. A man was arrested." | Oct 2017: Facebook's automatic translation rendered a Palestinian construction worker's Arabic "good morning" as "attack them" (Hebrew) / "hurt them" (English); Israeli police arrested him and released him hours later (Haaretz; AI Incident Database #72) | Shows "manageable" categories can still hurt one person. |
| "Aircraft. One bad sensor, and the seven-three-seven MAX's automation kept forcing the nose down. Three hundred forty-six people died." | Lion Air 610 (Oct 2018, 189 killed) and Ethiopian 302 (Mar 2019, 157 killed): MCAS relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor and repeatedly commanded nose-down trim on erroneous data | |
| "Power grids. A software bug silenced the alarms. A local fault became a blackout. Fifty million people lost power." | 2003 Northeast blackout: a race-condition bug in FirstEnergy's GE XA/21 alarm software stalled the alarms; the US–Canada Task Force final report (2004) says the blackout affected an estimated 50 million people (other tallies give ~55 million: 45 M US + 10 M Ontario) | We use the Task Force's 50 million, matching the source on the end card. | The bug was one cause among several (overgrown trees touching lines, operator and reliability-coordinator failures); the video says it silenced the alarms and that a local fault grew into the blackout, which is what happened, and does not claim the bug alone caused it. |
| "Weapons. A missile-defense clock drifted a third of a second. Twenty-eight soldiers died." | Patriot battery at Dhahran, 25 Feb 1991: after ~100 hours of continuous operation a time-conversion rounding error had accumulated to ~0.34 s, the range gate shifted ~687 m, the incoming Scud was not intercepted; 28 US soldiers were killed (GAO/IMTEC-92-26) | On screen "+0.34 s". |
| "Medicine. A radiation machine's software had bugs. Six patients got massive overdoses. At least three died." | Therac-25 (1985–87): race-condition software errors (more than one bug) (hardware interlocks removed) caused six known massive overdoses, some ~100x the intended dose; three of the injured patients later died of the overdose (Leveson & Turner, 1993; Wikipedia 'Therac-25') | |
| "The bigger the scale, and the higher the stakes, the smaller the mistake you can afford." | Synthesis of the brief | |
| Finale advice: start small before full rollout, let a person answer (appeal/override), watch for the mistake that keeps coming back, keep a stop button | Standard safety practice consistent with the MiDAS/Robodebt findings (no human review, no appeal, errors repeated for years) | Added: what to do. |
